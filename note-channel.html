来自做的几个思考题，我是纯纯新手

Question 1

We have $2^N$ types of N-bit strings form a total set called $A$. Then we randomly chose $2^{\alpha N}$ of them to form a subset of A,

$$S\subset A,\quad|S|=2^{\alpha N},\quad0\le\alpha\le 1$$

We randomly chose one element $x$ from S, for example,

$$x=0111011001...$$

Erase the first $\beta N$ bits of $x$ and we get $x'$ string, for example $\beta N=4$,

$$x'=????011001...$$

If one only know $x'$ and $S$, what's the success probability of guessing $x$?

Average probability

Define $y$ as the last $(1-\beta)N$ bits of $x'$. (As in the example, $y=011001...$)

For a certain $S$ and $x'$, define all the possible number of $x$,

$$M_{y}=\#\{x\in S:last~(1-\beta)N~bits~of~x=y\}$$

This gives us the number of correct answer. (If $M_y=1$, then fortunately we have 100% success probability.) If we guess randomly, then obviously the success probability is,

$$P(X|Y=y)=\frac{1}{M_y}$$

Now we average $P(X|Y=y)$ of $y$,

$$P_{suc}=\sum_{y}P(Y=y)\frac{1}{M_y}$$

where $P(Y=y)$ is equal to the number of strings that last bits are y, devides the number of $S$,

$$P(Y=y)=\frac{M_y}{2^{\alpha N}}$$

Hence,

$$P_{suc}=\frac{number~of~different~y}{2^{\alpha N}}:=\frac{B}{2^{\alpha N}}$$

We also want to talk about $B$ in a average meaning. Since we chose $S$ from $A$ randomly, there's no preference on what $y$ should be. Total number that a $(1-\beta)N$ bits string, $y$, should be $2^{(1-\beta)N}$. $2^{\alpha N}$ strings will choose them randomly.

It's something like **geometric distribution**. Each of the strings in $S$ chose one types of "last $(1-\beta)N$ bits" in a probability of $1/2^{(1-\beta)N}$, so after all strings chosen their last bits, the possibility of one types of "last $(1-\beta)N$ bits" that doesn't appear in $S$ is

$$P(doesn't~ ~appear)=(1-\frac{1}{2^{(1-\beta)N}})^{2^{\alpha N}}$$

Conversely, one type appear in $S$,

$$P(appear)=1-(1-\frac{1}{2^{(1-\beta)N}})^{2^{\alpha N}}$$

Now we have total $2^{(1-\beta)N}$ independent numbers of them, so the average number of different y is,

$$B=2^{(1-\beta)N}P(appear)=2^{(1-\beta)N}\left(1-(1-\frac{1}{2^{(1-\beta)N}})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)$$

Substitute this $B$ into $P_{suc}$, then we have the average guessing success probability:

$$\boxed{P_{suc}=\frac{2^{(1-\beta)N}}{2^{\alpha N}}\left(1-(1-\frac{1}{2^{(1-\beta)N}})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)}$$

Critical $\beta$ Under TDL

This is pure calculation or mathematics.

What we need to do now is to take $N\to\infty$ limit on $P_{suc}$:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}\left(1-(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)$$

Notice that

$$(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}=1-2^{\alpha N}2^{(\beta-1)N}+O((2^{\alpha N}2^{(\beta-1)N})^2)$$

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1>0$

Then $2^{(\alpha+\beta-1)N}\to\infty$, take ln:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}=\exp(\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{\alpha N}\ln(1-2^{(\beta-1)N}))=\exp(-\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(\alpha+\beta-1)N})=e^{-\infty}=0$$

So

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}\left(1-(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}=0$$

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{suc}=0$$

We almost can't guess the right answer.

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1=0$

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}\left(1-(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)=1-(1-\frac{1}{2^{\alpha N}})^{2^{\alpha N}}=1-\frac{1}{e}$$

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1<0$

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}\left(1-(1-2^{(\beta-1)N})^{2^{\alpha N}}\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}2^{(1-(\beta+\alpha))N}2^{\alpha N}2^{(\beta-1)N}=1$$

This means we have high probability to guess correctly.

From the limit calculation, we can find that the critical $\beta$ is $\boxed{\beta_{cric}=1-\alpha}$. When $\beta>\beta_{cric}$, too many information been discarded, the success probability drops to 0 exponentially.

Relation with Entropy

Intuition

If $\beta=0$ or $\alpha N=1$ (or $\alpha=0$ under TDL), we know the correct $x$ for sure. The smaller $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are, the more information we know.

The information of this game contains two steps, one lies in $S$, and the other lies in the last $(1-\beta)N$ bits. So $\alpha$ describes the information loss in step one, and $\beta$ describes the information loss in step two.

When the total information loss is small, that is $\alpha+\beta<1$, we can recover the original $x$. That's the meaning for $\beta_{cric}=1-\alpha$

Conditional Shannon Entropy

The posterior possibility is uniform,

$$P(x=X|y=Y)=\frac{1}{K_y}$$

So the conditional Shannon entropy is

$$H(X|Y=y)=\log_2M_y$$

Average on y:

$$H(X|Y)=\sum_{y}P(Y=y)\log_2M_y$$

While

$$P_{suc}=\sum_{y}P(Y=y)\frac{1}{M_y}$$

By Jensen inequality,

$$E[2^{-z}]\ge2^{-E[z]}$$

So we have

$$P_{suc}\ge 2^{-H(X|Y)}$$

Gives the lower bond of $P_{suc}$.

Moreover, under TDL, $2^{-H(X|Y)}$ is approximately $P_{suc}$.

For a certain $S$,

$$H(X|Y,S)=\sum_y P(Y=y|S)H(X|Y=y)=\log_2M_y=\frac{1}{2^{\alpha N}}\sum_y M_y\log_2M_y$$

Now we take average on $M_y$. Before it, we need to know what's the possibility that subset $S$ contains $m$ strings which share the same last $(1-\beta)N$ bits. Except the certain answer, others obey the Hypergeometric distribution, that is,

$$P(M_y=m)=\frac{C_{2^{\beta N}-1}^{m-1}C_{2^N-2^{\beta N}}^{2^{\alpha N}-m}}{C_{2^N-1}^{2^{\alpha N}-1}}$$

So

$$\bar{H}(X|Y)=\sum_{m=1}^{2^{\alpha N}}P(M_y=m)H(X|Y=y)=\sum_{m=1}^{2^{\alpha N}}\frac{C_{2^{\beta N}-1}^{m-1}C_{2^N-2^{\beta N}}^{2^{\alpha N}-m}}{C_{2^N-1}^{2^{\alpha N}-1}}\log_2M_y$$

We know that when $N\to\infty$, Hypergeometric distribution approximate to Poisson distribution, which has $\lambda=(2^{\alpha N}-1)\frac{2^{\beta N}-1}{2^N-1}\approx 2^{(\alpha+\beta-1)N}$

So when $N\to\infty$,

$$\bar{H}(X|Y)=\sum_{m=0}^\infty e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^m}{m!}\log_2(m+1)$$

Classical on $\lambda=2^{(\alpha+\beta-1)N}$

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1>0\Rightarrow\lambda\to\infty$

$$\bar{H}(X|Y)=\log_2(2^{(\alpha+\beta-1)N})+O(\frac{1}{\lambda})\to\infty$$

So

$$2^{-\bar{H}(X|Y)}\to 0=P_{suc}$$

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1=0\Rightarrow\lambda=1$

$$\bar{H}(X|Y)=\sum_{m=0}^\infty e^{-1}\frac{\log_2(m+1)}{m!}$$

Since $\frac{\log_2(m+1)}{m!}\le\frac{m}{m!}$, the series converge.

  1. $\alpha+\beta-1<0\Rightarrow\lambda\to0$

$$\bar{H}(X|Y)=0,\quad 2^{-\bar{H}(X|Y)}=1$$

This agrees with $P_{suc}=1$.

Rényi-2 Entropy

Rényi-2 entropy is defined by

$$H_2(X)=-\log_2\left(\sum_i p_i^2\right)$$

It measures the collision probability, $2^{H_2(X)}$ means there's how many effective same probability state in one distribution.

Relative Rényi-2 entropy is

$$H_2(X|Y)=-\log_2\left(\sum_yP(y)\sum_x P^2(x|y)\right)$$

In our model,

$$\sum_x P^2(x|y)=P(X|Y=y)=\frac{1}{M_y}$$

because we guess the answer randomly. So

$$\boxed{H_2(X|Y)=-\log_2\left(P_{suc}\right)}$$

It means when we guess randomly, the successful probability is described by Rényi-2 entropy, which represents the collision pairs.

Physical Application

In paper "Quantum resource localizability transitions in deep thermalization", I found the physical version of this pure informational question, that is theory of resource localization in deep thermalization for subspace-coherence QRTs.

The informational transition question is an easier case of random subset intersection problem in QRT. We view one string in $A$ as a block, which has uniform distribution $p=2^{-N}$ (block probability), then

$$H_2(p)=-\log_2(2^N2^{-2N})=N$$

By definition,

$$\sigma=\frac{H_2(p)}{N}=1$$

$\alpha_0 N$-qubits are put in resourceful basis, this is correspond to subset $S$, which $|S|=2^{\alpha N}$.

Then $\alpha_m N$-qubits are put in resourceful measurement basis, corresponds to $\beta N$. $2^{\beta N}$ is the number of all possible answer of string $x$.

This paper talks about whether the intersection of these two sets $I∩F$ is small enough to infer the original block.

Total number of collision can be approximate as below:

$$|I||F|\sim 2^{(\alpha_0+\alpha_m)N}$$

By Rényi-2 entropy, the number of effective collision pairs is

$$2^{H_2}=2^{\sigma N}$$

Now we compare $2^{(\alpha_0+\alpha_m)N}$ and $2^{\sigma N}$ , if the possible pairs of collision is restricted, that is $2^{(\alpha_0+\alpha_m)N}<2^{\sigma N}$, or $\alpha_0+\alpha_m<\sigma$, we can recover the original block, otherwise we can't do it under TDL.

The critical point is

$$\alpha_0+\alpha_m=\sigma$$

This is similar to the critical point in the informational guessing game:

$$\alpha+\beta=1$$

where $\alpha\leftrightarrow\alpha_0,~\beta\leftrightarrow\alpha_m,~\sigma=1$

notion: In this note, H(p) ($1\ge p\ge 1$ is a probability) means entropy in B(1,p) model, i.e. $H(p)=-p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)$

Question 2

In measurement decoding noise, let's model $P(s|z) \propto$ $e^{-\beta(s\oplus~z)}$ (bit-string addition). $\beta$ is the strength of measurement ranging from 0 to infinity. This is called a weak measurement (why?). For $\beta = \infty$ this is a strong measurement. What is the critical $\beta$ now in this model? What is the channel capacity?

Binary Symmetric Channel

Actually it's a product channel, each bit errors are independent:

$$P(s|z)\propto\prod_{i=1}^Ne^{-\beta(s_i\oplus z_i)}$$

where $s=s_1s_2...s_N,~s_i\in\{0,1\}$, same for $z$. So we can look at only one bit behavior to understand this channel:

$$P(s_i\neq z_i)\propto e^{-\beta},\quad P(s_i= z_i)\propto 1$$

This is like 2-energy system, where $E_0=0$ and $E_1=1$. We can also regard this bit flip error as original bit $\oplus$ random error bit:

$$z_i=s_i\oplus r_i,\quad P(r_i=0)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta}},\quad P(r_i=1)=\frac{e^{-\beta}}{1+e^{-\beta}}$$

$r_i$ obeys Boltzmann distribution under reversed temperature $\beta$. In zero temperature, $P(r_i=0)=1$, the channel is accurate (strong measurement); In infinite temperature, $P(r_i=0)=P(r_i=1)=\frac{1}{2}$, the channel is totally random, code word $z$ carries no information, we can't decide the answer unless $2^{\alpha N}=1$ (or $\alpha=0$)

$\beta$ controls the error rate, $\beta<\infty$ it is called weak measurement since the code word $s$ we got is not always the original code word $z$

This is the binary symmetric channel, with bit flip probability

$$p=\frac{e^{-\beta}}{1+e^{-\beta}}$$

Fano Inequality

Message X, come from set $S$ randomly ($|S|=M$), passed through a BSC, and received $Y$

Guessing $X'$ from Y, let decoding error probability is $P_{err}=P(X'\neq X)$. Then Fano inequality says,

$$H(X|Y)\le H(P_{err})+P_{err}\log_2(M-1)$$

In this model, information X are uniformly taken from S, which means $H(X)=\log_2 M$, so

$$H(X)-H(X|Y)\ge -H(P_{err})+(1-P_{err})\log_2M$$

Substitute $\log_2 M=\alpha N,~I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y)$ into it:

$$I(X;Y)\ge -H(P_{err})+(1-P_{err})\alpha N$$

$$\frac{I(X;Y)}{N}\ge -\frac{H(P_{err})}{N}+(1-P_{err})\alpha$$

Under large N limit, $H(P_{err})/N\to0$, so

$$P_{err}\ge 1-\frac{I(X;Y)}{\alpha N}$$

Successfully decoding means $P_{err}=0$, so

$$\boxed{\frac{I(X;Y)}{N}\ge\alpha}$$

$I(X;Y)/N$ means the information you get from Y (in order to infer X) per bit. It can not be smaller than total information (per bit) $\alpha$, if you want to decode successfully.

Maximum Likelihood Decoding

Since flip probability $p\le\frac{1}{2}$, and

$$P(Y=y|X=x)=p^{d(x,y)}(1-p)^{N-d(x,y)}$$

where $d(·,·)$ represent for Hamming distance, our decoding rule is to find $x'\in S$ letting $d(x',y)$ to be the smallest.

$$\min_{x\in S}d(x,y)=d(x',y)$$

For N-bit code word $s$, after passing through BSC, how many distance will the BSC make? Recall $z=s\oplus r$, $d(z,s)=d(r)$, and

$$d(r)\sim B(N,p)$$

Big number theory tells us for arbitrary $\delta>0$, we have

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}P\left(d(r)>(1+\delta)Np\right)=0$$

So if we can show that no other code word in radius $Np$ Hamming ball $B_H(Np)$, our maximum likelihood decoder is success.

$$|B_H(Np)|=\sum_{i=1}^{Np}C_N^i$$

Need to get the upper bond of $|B_H(Np)|$, this process is pure calculation.
Binomial expansion gets its maximum in the 1-p side, so
$$p^i(1-p)^{N-i}\ge p^{Np}(1-p)^{N-Np},\quad1\le i\le Np\le N/2$$
So
$$1=\sum_{i=1}^N C_N^i p^i(1-p)^{N-i}\ge\sum_{i=1}^{Np} C_N^i p^i(1-p)^{N-i}\ge\sum_{i=1}^{Np} C_N^i p^{Np}(1-p)^{N-Np}$$
$$1\ge|B_H(Np)|p^{Np}(1-p)^{N-Np}$$
$$|B_H(Np)|\le[p^{-p}(1-p)^{-(1-p)}]^N=2^{NH(p)}$$

Obtained this upper bond

$$|B_H(Np)|\le 2^{NH(p)}$$

Total code word $2^N$, one code word enters into this Hamming ball with probability $P_1=2^{(H(p)-1)N}$

Possibility that code word in S enter in this Hamming ball is

$$P_{err}\le2^{\alpha N}P_1=2^{(\alpha+H(p)-1)N}$$

When $1-H(p)>\alpha~\Rightarrow~P_{err}=0$, decode success.

Critical Point

We know:

$$\alpha<1-H(p),\quad success$$

$$\alpha>\frac{1}{N}(H(Y)-H(Y|X)),\quad failure$$

Are there any connection between these two bond? Answer is: they can be set to equal, and the value is capacity.

For one bit,

$$P(x_i\neq y_i)=p,\quad P(x_i= y_i)=1-p$$

So

$$H(Y|X)=\sum_{i=1}^N P(x_i=1)H(y_i|x_i=1)+P(x_i=0)H(y_i|x_i=0)=NH(p)$$

For one bit, $H(y_i)\le1$. This can be taken by setting $P(x_i=1)=P(x_i=0)=\frac{1}{2}$:

$$H(y_i)_{P(x_i=0)=0.5}=H(\frac{1}{2}p+\frac{1}{2}(1-p))=H(\frac{1}{2})=1$$

This step means we modify the original information probability when using this BSC to make higher efficiency. Since the channel is symmetric, the optimal probability is $P(x_i=1)=P(x_i=0)=\frac{1}{2}$.

Then the failure condition becomes:

$$\alpha>1-H(p),\quad failure$$

There's no gap between successful bond and failure bond, so we can ensure the transition happens in

$$\boxed{H(p)_{cric}+\alpha=1}$$

Substitute

$$p=\frac{e^{-\beta}}{1+e^{-\beta}},\quad 1-p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta}}$$

We can rewrite the critical point using $\beta_c$:

$$\frac{e^{-\beta_c}}{1+e^{-\beta_c}}\log_2\frac{1+e^{-\beta_c}}{e^{-\beta_c}}+\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta_c}}\log_2(1+e^{-\beta_c})=1-\alpha$$

Channel Capacity

The meaning of channel capacity is $C=$ bits/channel used. In other word, using N times of this channel, CN bits can be decoded with success probability $P_{suc}\to1$ when $N\to\infty$

As the discussion above, since information per bit we want to transmit is $\alpha$, the capacity should be $C=1-H(p)$. From Fano inequality to $C$, we have taken $\max H(Y)$. So generally,

$$C=\max_{P(X)}\frac{I(X;Y)}{N}$$

Meaning: when channel capacity > information density, we can decode it successfully.

n-Copies

If we discard first $\beta N$ bits, then n-copies raise n times of exactly same $z$, it will not let the system distinguishable.

But in BSC, it do have a better effect.

Majority Vote

For flip probability $p<\frac{1}{2}$, if we receive 0,0,1,0,0,0 (n=6) in one bit, then we want to guess this bit to be 0 naturally. This method can let success probability higher.

The information procedure in majority vote method is

$$original~string~X\xrightarrow{BSC^n}Y_1,...,Y_n\xrightarrow{vote}\bar{Y}\xrightarrow{max~likelihood}X'$$

From X to Y forms a new BSC, now we calculate the new flip probability $q_n$. Majority vote is wrong means there's more than $\frac{n}{2}$ bit flip happens in original BSC(p):

$$q_n=\sum_{i=n/2}^nC_n^ip^i(1-p)^{n-i}

Using this decoding method, the critical point shift to

$$\boxed{H(q_n)_{cric}+\alpha=1}$$

Capacity

Majority vote is not the optimal decoding method, it can only gives lower bond of successful probability. By calculating capacity C, we will know the optimal transition point.

By definition, $C=\max\frac{I(X;Y)}{N}$. Here the channel is symmetric again, so $I/N$ gets its maximum when $P(x_i=0)=P(x_i=1)=0.5,~H(Y)=1$, consider one bit:

$$C=1-H(X|Y^n)$$$$H(X|Y^n)=\sum_{y^n}P(Y^n=y^n)H(P(X=0|Y^n=y^n))$$

Let us consider $y^n=1,1,...,0,0..,0$, which first k times we receive 1, and last n-k times we receive 0. Then

$$P(y^n|X=0)=p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$

$$P(y^n|X=1)=p^{n-k}(1-p)^k$$

$$P(X=0|y^n)=\frac{P(X=0,y^n)}{P(y^n)}=\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}}{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}$$

For symmetric information, if there are k times of 0 appear in $y^n$, then the probability is

$$P(Y^n=y^n)=\frac{1}{2}P(y^n|X=0)+\frac{1}{2}P(y^n|X=1)=\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}{2}$$

$$H(X|Y^n)=\sum_{y^n\in 2^n}\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}{2}H\left(\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}}{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}\right)$$

Summation over k instead of $y^n$, which yields a $C_n^k$ factor:

$$H(X|Y^n)=\sum_{k=0}^nC_n^k\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}{2}H\left(\frac{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}}{p^k(1-p)^{n-k}+p^{n-k}(1-p)^k}\right)$$

The optimal critical point is

$$\boxed{H(X|Y^n)_{cric}+\alpha=1}$$

Intuitionally, when n gets bigger, critical $\beta_{cric}$ gets smaller, and $n\to\infty\Rightarrow\beta_{cric}\to0$

Distinguishability

System will have higher distinguishability, because the posterior distribution become sharpener. (For quick verification, $P(y^n|X=0):P(y^n|X=1)=(\frac{1-p}{p})^{n-2k}$, hold $\frac{k}{n}$ unchanged, let $n\to\infty$, this ratio approaches to $\infty$) Specifically, the posterior probably of right answer approaches to 1.

It makes $H(X|Y^n)$ smaller, and channel capacity $C=\frac{1}{N}I(X;Y)$ higher.

Identity RCA

We use this example to explicitly show some kind of "update rule" will make the code undistinguishable.

Consider identity RCA, set $\alpha=\frac{1}{2}$ and $N=4$, then S contains:

$$S=\{0000,0001,0010,0011\}$$

  1. Erase last 2 bits channel:

This will let any information become "00" —— totally undistinguishable.

  1. Erase first 2 bits channel:

This is the best channel we can use, making decode success. Here $\beta=\frac{1}{2}$, $\beta+\alpha=1$ —— still restricted under capacity condition.

  1. Bit flip noise channel:

S only contains a small square of N-bit codes. The maximum Hamming distance is 2, less than maximum N-bit code distance 4. This RCA makes BSC efficiency lower.

Property

RCA will let the difference between two original stings propagate to other position.

For example, if two stings only have one bit difference, and unfortunately it located in first $\beta N$ bits, then they are undistinguishable through erase channel. However after RCA evolve, there's new difference in last $(1-\beta)N$ bits, they will become distinguishable.

For BSC, a good RCA will make $\min_{x,y\in S}d(x,y)$ higher, in order to create more tolerance space.

We can evaluate if one RCA can do better than random permutation for a certain channel. We can carefully pick the optimal RCA to create more tolerance space. But Shannon capacity condition $C\ge\alpha$ is always true.

Advantages

RCA is local, we have a specific rule, not random, so we can save the RAM.

Disadvantages

One step of RCA only affects $O(1)$ of bit position, but random permutation can affect O(N)

Quantum Error Correction

How to encode quantum information, hide it, corrupt it, yet still recover it. This is quantum error correction ;)

I learned something in part one of "Surviving as a Quantum Computer in a Classical World" wrote by Daniel Gottesman.

For example, 9-qubits

$$\begin{aligned} |0\rangle \mapsto |\overline{0}\rangle &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle + |111\rangle)(|000\rangle + |111\rangle)(|000\rangle + |111\rangle) \\ |1\rangle \mapsto |\overline{1}\rangle &= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}(|000\rangle - |111\rangle)(|000\rangle - |111\rangle)(|000\rangle - |111\rangle). \end{aligned}$$

It can solve bit flip and phase flip errors.

Stabilizer of nine-qubit code:

$$\begin{equation*}

\begin{array}{ccccccccc}

Z & Z & & & & & & & \\

& Z & Z & & & & & & \\

& & & Z & Z & & & & \\

& & & & Z & Z & & & \\

& & & & & & Z & Z & \\

& & & & & & & Z & Z \\

X & X & X & X & X & X & & & \\

& & & X & X & X & X & X & X

\end{array}

\end{equation*}$$

Stabilizer of five-qubit code:

$$\begin{matrix} X & Z & Z & X & I \\ I & X & Z & Z & X \\ X & I & X & Z & Z \\ Z & X & I & X & Z \end{matrix}$$

We can use projector to obtain $\ket{\bar{0}}$ and $\ket{\bar{1}}$, they are long.

Both 9-qubit code and 5-qubit code have one logical qubit. Define $N(S)$ as below:

$$N(S)=\{N\in P_N|NM=MN\quad\forall M\in S\}$$

Then stabilizer can't distinguish errors in $\hat{\mathsf{N}}(\mathsf{S}) \setminus \hat{\mathsf{S}}$ (hat means omit phase).

Stabilizer has two dimension symmetric representation, which could let us consider stabilizer questions under liner algebra schemes.
